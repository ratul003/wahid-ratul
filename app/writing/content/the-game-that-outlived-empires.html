"Football is not a matter of life and death. It is much more important than that." — Bill Shankly

There is a photograph I keep coming back to. Black-and-white, 1950, somewhere in Rio. A barefoot boy, eight or nine, kicking a ball of rolled-up newspaper and tape. He is alone and entirely content. His face has the focus of a child working on the only problem that matters to him.

Nobody wrote down his name. He is just a kid with a rag ball, in a city about to pack two hundred thousand people into a stadium for the same game he is playing in the dirt.

That is the whole sport, in a single frame. It asks nothing of you and gives back everything.

This is about that game. And about what is being done to it.

I. The Beautiful Accident

FIFA was founded in 1904. Seven countries. A handshake. A belief that football could cross borders without going to war.

The early administrators accidentally built something they didn't fully understand: a controlled arena where the human need to beat someone else could happen with a ball rather than a weapon. Countries that had spent centuries trying to kill each other could compete for something real, with rules both sides agreed to, in front of witnesses. The loser went home. Nobody annexed anything.

Then 1934 arrived. Mussolini used the tournament as propaganda. Italy won under institutional pressure. The playbook was written early: host the Cup, shape the conditions, collect the legitimacy. Nobody retired that playbook. It was just refined.

Cold War, Iron Curtain, One Ball

The 1966 World Cup drew 400 million viewers, the first truly global broadcast sporting event. The 1970 tournament introduced colour television to hundreds of millions of homes at once.

Brazil defeats Italy 4–1. Estadio Azteca, Mexico City, June 21, 1970. This image went around the world in colour. There are people alive who describe it as the moment they understood what television was for. Public Domain.

Hungary and West Germany were divided by everything the Cold War could throw at the two countries. They still shared ninety minutes of football. It wasn't peace. But it was a rehearsal for it.

The World Cup didn't just teach the world to watch sports together. It taught the world to watch. And the more people watched, the more there was to sell.

II. How Late-Stage Capitalism Is Ruining the Sport

Start with Chuck Blazer

Chuck Blazer ran CONCACAF, the football federation for North and Central America, from Trump Tower in New York. He rented two floors: one for himself, one for his cats. Both are billed to CONCACAF's operating budget.

For years, he attended FIFA meetings wearing a recording device hidden in a keychain fob. He was cooperating with the FBI. When the Department of Justice indictment landed in 2015, Blazer was named as a co-conspirator who had accepted bribes for World Cup hosting votes. He died in 2017. He was never imprisoned.

Start with Chuck Blazer because he is the most human version of what FIFA became. The money got so large, and the accountability so absent, that an official could rent a floor of Trump Tower for his cats and nobody stopped him.

FIFA's revenue from Qatar 2022: $7.5 billion. In 1990, the total World Cup prize money was $56 million. By 2022, $440 million.

Sepp Blatter. Five consecutive presidential elections. Presided over the most corrupt era in world football administration. Suspended from football in 2015. Still appealing that ban years later. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

The 2015 indictment ran to 47 counts and $150 million in documented bribes. The Michael Garcia report, 430 pages investigating the 2018 and 2022 hosting bids, was summarised by FIFA. Garcia publicly stated the summary was inaccurate. He resigned in protest. The full report was suppressed for years.

Qatar: The Full Picture

Lusail Stadium. Built at a cost exceeding $700 million. Air-conditioned. The 2022 World Cup Final was played here. The migrant workers who built it are not credited anywhere on the structure. Djawed_vlog, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0

Eight stadiums in a country where summer temperatures exceed 45°C. Built by migrant workers under the kafala system, which legally bound workers to their employers, and made leaving, complaining, or refusing unsafe conditions effectively impossible.

The Guardian's 2021 investigation estimated 6,500 migrant workers from India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka had died since the bid was awarded in 2010. Some of them were in their twenties. Their names are not on the stadium.

FIFA made $7.5 billion. Qatar's international reputation improved measurably. The investment return per dollar spent was, by any metric, extraordinary.

Football kept going. It always does.

Oil Money and the European Infection

In 2017, PSG paid €222 million for Neymar. More than double any previous transfer fee. Several journalists assumed it was a typo.

Neymar's PSG presentation, August 2017. Qatar Sports Investments triggered his €222 million release clause, still the most expensive transfer in football history. Antoine Dellenbach, Wikimedia Commons.jpg), CC BY-SA 2.0

Qatar Sports Investments bought PSG in 2011. Manchester City was acquired by Abu Dhabi in 2008, and by 2023 faced 115 Premier League charges for alleged financial rule breaches. Newcastle was sold to Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund in 2021: the same vehicle that funds LIV Golf, backs the 2034 Saudi hosting bid, and operates inside a state whose operatives murdered a journalist in its own consulate in Istanbul in 2018.

These are not separate stories. Authoritarian states discovered that buying a football club delivers more reputational return per dollar than any diplomatic campaign. A Champions League final reaches 450 million viewers. A state visit gets a handshake and a press pool.

We are still watching. The fairy tales require more setup now. The feeling that any given match could go any way, that feeling is smaller than it was. And the people who profited from its erosion are comfortable.

And yet I still watch. Everyone who loves this game has a reason they can't quite justify. Mine is Germany.

III. Why Germany

Why I Support Them

I did not inherit Germany. No one in my family is German, and I have never set foot in the country. I chose them, the way you choose the thing that names something in you before you can explain it.

What I chose was the ruthlessness. Germany is the only team in World Cup history that turns the biggest matches into executions. Not narrow wins, demolitions. In 2010, they put four past England in the round of 16 and four past Argentina, Messi's Argentina, in the quarter-final, both on the counter, both finished before the opponent understood what was happening. In 2014, they scored seven against Brazil, in Brazil, five of them inside eighteen first-half minutes. No other nation has that habit. Brazil has flair, Italy has defence, Spain has the ball. Germany has the kill.

A turnover becomes a shot before the block resets, the German method behind 4–1 England, 4–0 Argentina, 7–1 Brazil.

The skeleton is always the same. Win the ball high, go vertical at once, arrive at the goal in two or three passes before the shape can reform. Kimmich drops and breaks a line with one pass, Wirtz and Musiala thread the gaps, and the striker is already running. It is not beautiful the way Brazil is beautiful. It is beautiful the way a proof is beautiful: every part does exactly one job, and the conclusion is a goal.

That is what I support. Not a flag. A method.

1954: The Miracle That Made a Nation

West Germany should not have won the 1954 World Cup. Hungary were unbeaten in four years. They'd destroyed England 6–3 at Wembley, the first foreign team ever to win there. They had beaten West Germany 8–3 in the group stage two weeks before the final.

The final ended 3–2 to West Germany. Helmut Rahn scored twice. Hungary hit the post in the dying minutes. The better team lost.

But this wasn't really about who was better.

West Germany in 1954 was nine years from unconditional surrender. Cities still being rebuilt. A population still processing what the regime before them had done and allowed. National pride lived in a complicated, guilt-laden space, not absent, but impossible to express the way another nation might have been.

Das Wunder von Bern, the Miracle of Bern, gave West Germany a clean version of that pride. A victory completely disconnected from conquest or ideology. Just a ball, a line, a whistle. We can win at something. We can, in some limited but human sense, be great again.

Football and national recovery were bound together in the German story in ways that still echo today.

1974 and 1990: Two More Stars

Franz Beckenbauer, 1972, two years before he lifted the World Cup on home soil. He invented the attacking libero and changed how the game understood defence. Sport 6, März 1972. Public Domain.

West Germany hosted the 1974 World Cup and won it, 2–1 over the Netherlands: Beckenbauer sweeping from the back, Gerd Müller lurking in the box in that way that made defenders feel something was about to happen even when it wasn't. Müller retired at 30 because he felt like it.

Sixteen years later, they did it again, against a backdrop no script would dare. The Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989; eight months later, on July 8, 1990, Brehme's penalty beat Argentina in Rome and Matthäus lifted the trophy. The reunification paperwork was finalised three months later. The trophy came first.

November 9, 1989, the Brandenburg Gate. Eight months later, West Germany lifted the World Cup, the last time the country that won it still legally existed. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

My Birth Year, and What Came After

I was born in 1996. Germany won the Euros that summer at Wembley. Oliver Bierhoff scored the first golden goal in major tournament history, the match over at once, the trophy theirs.

I know this is the way you know any fact from before your conscious memory: retroactively meaningful.

Euro 2000 broke the old model. Group stage exit. 3–0 to Portugal. The DFB looked at the system that had produced four World Cups and two European Championships and made a decision almost no sporting organisation ever has the courage to make: this is broken. We rebuild from scratch.

Mandatory youth academies at every Bundesliga club. A rewritten coaching curriculum. A decade of waiting for a pipeline they couldn't yet see.

The result was 2014.

The 7–1, the Götze Moment, a Man in Tears

The demolition of Brazil at the Mineirão on July 8, 2014, is the greatest hour of football I have ever watched.

I was 18, and I watched it alone in my bedroom like I was eight. Germany were 5–0 inside 29 minutes. Müller, Klose, Kroos twice in 178 seconds, Khedira. Then Schürrle, twice more. 7–1. The score doesn't tell you anything. A score can't.

2026: The Machine, Rebuilt

Russia 2018 was a structural collapse. Qatar 2022 was better. A quarterfinal, real regeneration, a team becoming something. But not yet.

Now: Florian Wirtz at 22, a club-record move to Liverpool behind him and one of the best young creative players in the world. Jamal Musiala is already among the best young players on earth. Manuel Neuer, 40 and out of international retirement, asked to hold the line one last time despite a season that has shown his age. A press that took Curaçao apart 7–1 in the opener, the win that made Germany the World Cup's all-time top scorers.

The statement was made early.

IV. The Model: A Live Simulation, Built in R

"In God we trust. All others must bring data" — W. Edwards Deming

Everything so far has been a story. This part is a method. I wanted to know, not feel, know, what the 2026 World Cup actually looks like as a probability space, so I built one: a Monte Carlo engine in R, wrapped in a Shiny application, wired to a live results feed so the predictions move with the tournament. What follows is the whole thing, opened up. If you are the kind of reader who trusts a claim more once you've seen the equations and the assumptions behind it, this section is for you.

Why simulate, instead of predict?

A single prediction is a guess wearing a confident voice. "France wins" is right about one tournament and silent about the other four in five. Football is structured uncertainty: talent is real, but so is the hot goalkeeper, the soft penalty in the 119th minute, the group of death that eats a favourite alive.

So the engine never answers "who wins?" It answers the honest question: across all the futures that could plausibly happen, how is the space of outcomes distributed? It plays the entire tournament a million times, lets probability resolve every match, and reports the shape of the results. A team is the favourite because it wins most of the universes, not all of them.

Why these models, and not the others

Every choice below is a fork, and at each one, there was a more fashionable option I turned down on purpose.

Monte Carlo, not a single prediction. "France will win" is a sentence, not a model. A tournament is a branching tree, 104 matches each conditional on the last. Only simulation walks every branch and reports the distribution, including the rare paths where a favourite dies in the group of death.

Elo, not bookmaker odds or a black-box rating. Elo is transparent, self-updating, and needs no training corpus, three things international football demands, because nations play rarely and the data is thin. Bookmaker odds are sharper, but they are a black box I cannot take apart on a page.

Poisson goals, not win/draw/loss. A logistic win-probability model throws away the scoreline, and the scoreline is what decides groups (goal difference, goals for) and what produces a believable 7–1. Mapping strength to an expected-goals rate and simulating the score keeps that.

Negative Binomial in the knockouts, not Poisson everywhere. Plain Poisson forces variance to equal the mean and quietly understates upsets. One-off knockout football is noisier than that, so knockout goals come from a Negative Binomial (a Gamma-mixed Poisson) whose extra dispersion is exactly where giant-killings live.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck momentum is not a random walk. Form is real but temporary. A mean-reverting process lets a team carry heat into the next round and then cool off, instead of a random walk where one good night compounds forever.

Bayesian head-to-head priors, not pure independence. Some rivalries don't run to form. A light log-odds nudge lets those patterns recur at their historical rate instead of being averaged into nothing.

What I avoided on purpose: a neural net (no labelled international dataset large enough, and no interpretability), a pure xG model (needs shot-level data that doesn't exist before kick-off), and simply copying the bookmakers (accurate, and opaque). The goal was never the most accurate black box. It was a model where every number traces back to an assumption you can see and argue with.

The data layer: Elo ratings, and a live feed

The structural input is each nation's World Football Elo rating Rᵢ. Elo is a sufficient statistic for baseline strength: a gap of Δ R maps to a win expectation through the logistic,

so a 200-point edge is the familiar ≈ 76%. The app does not treat ratings as frozen. On every load, it polls a live World Cup 2026 results feed; for each finished match, it applies an online Elo update

and recomputes the strength terms below. Germany thrashes Curaçao 7–1 in the opener, and their rating and their simulated title odds rise before your next run. The prediction is alive.

From rating to goals: the Poisson core

Most public models stop at win/draw/loss. That throws away the scoreline, and the scoreline is what decides groups (goal difference, goals for) and what produces realistic blowouts. So strength is mapped to an expected-goals rate, and the score is simulated.

Form, fatigue, and momentum

The adjuster M_A is where a tournament gets texture. It bundles pre-tournament club form, a fatigue penalty scaled by minutes played, a live group-stage goal-difference term, and an in-tournament momentum variable f that follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, the continuous-time cousin of a first-order autoregressive process, where shocks from wins and losses decay rather than persist forever:

A team that wins ugly carries a little heat into the next round; a team that gets humbled cools off. Neither lasts the whole cup.

Knockouts: overdispersion and penalties

One-off knockout matches are noisier than the group stage, variance exceeds the mean, so a plain Poisson understates upsets. Knockout goals are therefore drawn from a Negative-Binomial (a Gamma-mixed Poisson), with the dispersion r tightening each round as the surviving field strengthens:

History as a prior: the head-to-head matrix

Strength rating alone assumes every match is independent of the past. It isn't: Brazil keeps losing to Europe, Germany keeps reaching finals, and certain rivalries simply don't run to form. Each documented regularity enters as a Bayesian head-to-head prior h_AB, a log-odds nudge on the goal rate, so the pattern recurs at roughly its historical frequency instead of being averaged away. France carries h=+0.22 over Brazil; Germany +0.18. That France number is not arbitrary: they knocked Brazil out of the 1998 final and the 2006 quarter-final, and on strength rating alone, the match is near a coin flip, so the prior lifts France's win probability in that specific knockout several points above what their rating would grant. Most models average that pattern into nothing; this one is built to let it recur. The full antisymmetric matrix (h_BA=−h_AB) is visualised on the Ironies tab.

How it runs: the R / Shiny architecture

The application is a reactive pipeline:

```r # UI: sliders for simulations, factor weights, knockout pressure, form momentum # Server: a reactive simulation triggered on Run sim <- eventReactive(input$run, { W <- reactiveWeights() # form / squad / fixtures / tiredness / h2h elo <- update_elo(base_elo, live_results()) # online K=40 update alpha <- exp((elo - mean(elo)) / 450) beta <- exp(-(elo - mean(elo)) / 680) replicate(input$nsims, run_tournament(alpha, beta, W, H2H)) # vectorised }) live_results <- reactivePoll(..., fetch = function() fromJSON(api_url)) # httr + jsonlite output$bracket <- renderPlotly({ draw_bracket(sim()) }) ```

`run_tournament()` simulates 12 groups (`rpois`), ranks on points → goal difference → goals-for, seeds the round of 32, then plays five knockout rounds (`rnbinom`, penalty model) and returns the champion plus every round reached. `replicate()` Over a million calls give the full outcome distribution; `plotly` renders it; `reactivePoll()` keeps the Elo inputs synced to the live feed. UI weights are reactive, so moving a slider re-runs the engine, and every chart and the projected bracket redraw.

Methodology: Monte Carlo and convergence

The estimator is simple: the probability of any event (Germany reaching the final, USA winning the group) is its relative frequency across N simulated tournaments. By the law of large numbers, it converges on the model's true probability, and the Monte-Carlo standard error shrinks as 1/√N:

At N = 10⁶, a 12% probability carries a standard error near ± 0.03 percentage points, and the figures are stable to the first decimal, which is why the default run is a full million.

The assumptions, stated plainly

Every model is a set of assumptions; honesty is listing them.

  1. Goals are (Negative-Binomial-/Poisson-)distributed and conditionally independent given the two sides' strengths. Real matches have within-game correlation; the engine ignores it.
  1. Elo is a sufficient summary of baseline strength, and rating gaps map to expectations through the logistic above.
  1. No fixed host bonus. Home advantage enters only through live form and group goal difference, a deliberate choice for a three-host tournament.
  1. Head-to-head priors are stationary and act multiplicatively on the log goal-rate. They are calibrated to historical knockout records, not re-estimated mid-tournament.
  1. Form is mean-reverting (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) with independent normal shocks; momentum is real but temporary.
  1. Squad depth degrades roughly linearly with each knockout round (fatigue).
  1. Shootouts are a weighted coin from penalty history × depth × a small Elo term, not a kick-by-kick model.
  1. Online Elo uses a fixed K=40 and carries no rating uncertainty; the point estimate is treated as known.
  1. Qualification of the best third-placed teams and the bracket draw are approximated, not the exact FIFA seeding rules.
  1. Individual awards come from a separate player heuristic, not the match engine.

None of these is exotic; all of them are arguable. That is the point: they are visible, so you can disagree with a number because you can see why it is what it is.

What the simulation says: 1,000,000 tournaments

Title odds across 1,000,000 simulated tournaments, Germany in red. The top is flat, and the field is deep.

Argentina (12.6%) edges it: the highest rating, the deepest squad, and the psychology of a side that has already done this. France (12.1%) is a coin flip behind: fully fit, the lowest injury load in the field, structurally hard to beat. The gap between them is well inside the noise of a single tournament.

But the number that stops me sits in plain sight. The favourite wins only 12.6% of the time, which means the single most likely outcome of the whole tournament, by a distance, is not Argentina: 87 times in 100 the trophy goes to someone else. The entire top eight together account for only 63% of the titles, so more than a third of the time, the team lifting the cup is one nobody in this table picked. That is not a flaw in the model. It is the tournament.

Germany at 6.7% is what the numbers say, and notably higher than a rating-only model would grant, because the finals-machine prior is doing its work. What the numbers can't say is whether Wirtz has the run Müller had in 2014. In roughly one simulated tournament in five, Germany reaches the semi-final. That is nothing.

Closing

There is a version of football in 2026 that Sepp Blatter built. The 48 teams. The $7.5 billion. The sovereign wealth in the dressing rooms. The stadiums were built for a summer tournament that the host country's summers couldn't hold. The workers who died, whose names no official record has kept.

And there is the version the kid with the rag ball built. The 80th-minute goal nobody predicted. The goalkeeper nobody feared who saves two penalties in a shootout and sends a giant home. The team from a country nobody picked that wins their group and leaves everyone scrambling to redraw their brackets.

Lusail, December 18, 2022. Messi finally lifts it, his teammates around him. He scored twice in the final. Mbappe scored a hat-trick, it went to penalties, and Argentina won. Eight years after those tears at the Maracanã, the trophy is his. The photograph of him crying in 2014 and this one exist in the same life. Sebas / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.

Both versions are always in the same tournament. This is football's permanent contradiction, the thing that makes it impossible to fully love and impossible to stop watching.

In 67,000 of the million simulated universes, Germany lifts the trophy at MetLife Stadium, just outside New York, on July 19th.

The model says it's unlikely. History says Germany has done unlikely things before.

I was born in 1996. Germany won the Euros that summer. I didn't see it. But it was the world I was born into.

I'm watching now.
