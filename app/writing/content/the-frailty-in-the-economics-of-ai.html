The neon sign beside the stage said FULLY BOOKED.

It was lying.

It was a Thursday evening in late September, in Penang, in one of the best rated bars on the island. The chandelier was lit. The bottles ran the length of the wall, glowing red. Napkins stood folded in glasses on the tables, and on the little stage the music stands and a microphone had been set out for a band.

Every chair was empty. We were the first customers the bar had seen all day.

A quiet week

At 8PM on a Friday, in the highest rated restaurant in Penang, we were the only table. In Batu Ferringhi we were the only couple staying in the building, which is romantic for exactly one evening.

Kuala Lumpur had its quiet rooms too.

Up in the Cameron Highlands we stood in the middle of the main road at dusk to take a photograph, and nothing came.

Even the tea, in a tea plantation, had no queue.

Everywhere we walked there were To Let signs. It felt like a beautiful house with every light on and nobody home.

Two things happened to the traveller

I have an economics degree, so I drew a line from that bar to the world economy before the ice had melted. Here it is, as simply as I can draw it.

The trip got more expensive. When the war between the United States and Iran began, jet fuel in Singapore more than doubled in a week and hit a record, about $230 a barrel. The airlines passed it on.

And the traveller got poorer. This year technology companies cut more than 206,000 jobs while spending an estimated $700 billion on AI. One in five job cuts announced in the United States named AI as the reason.

A dearer ticket and a thinner wallet. You do not need a model for what happens next. You need a bar in Penang on a Thursday.

The chair I am sitting in

I would love to tell this as a story about other people. I cannot.

I run product operations at a startup, alone. This year, with AI doing the typing, I have been the product manager, the data scientist, the engineer, the designer, the analyst who checks the work, and three more besides. Last week I published an article boasting about it. A few years ago, I wrote, each of those was a different person.

Eight jobs. One chair.

Our service team handles 2.8 times the work it did in January, with the same nine or so people. Nobody was laid off. Nobody was hired either.

I keep thinking about the other seven chairs. The people who would have sat in them would have had salaries, and a week of leave, and somewhere they wanted to go. They are the second table in that restaurant. They are the audience for the band.

My laptop keeps the receipt. Four days of that help cost $114.37 at list price. No salary on earth competes with that, and I say so as someone who would like to keep his.

The oldest mistake in economics

There is a name for this, and it is older than any machine: the fallacy of composition. What is sensible for one is ruinous when everyone does it.

One company replacing a role with software is being careful. Every company doing it at once is deleting its own customers, because every wage saved was somebody else's revenue.

In the 1950s the union leader Walter Reuther was shown around a newly automated car plant. How, an executive asked him, will you get these machines to pay union dues? How, Reuther asked, will you get them to buy cars?

That is the frailty in the economics of AI. We are getting better at making things at exactly the rate we are getting worse at paying the people who buy them. It is progress. It is also a sword we are holding by the blade.

Why the bar goes first

Nobody cancels the rent to keep the holiday. Travel is what people buy with what is left over, so it is the first thing to go, and the places that live on it are the first to know.

In Malaysia that is more than one job in five.

In September, the month I was there, Malaysians flying around their own country were down 21.8% on a year earlier. That is the local middle class, the customer a hotel in Batu Ferringhi lives on, staying home.

The hotels kept building. But in the first half of 2026 barely half the rooms in the country were filled, 51.2%, down from 54.1% in the same months of 2025.

I cannot trace one lost job to one empty table, and nobody can. It does not arrive as a crash. It arrives as a Thursday.

The sign

We keep two ledgers, in different buildings. In one is the saving: the 2.8 times, the eight jobs in one chair, the $114. In the other is a room with the chandelier lit and a microphone waiting for a band. It is the same transaction, entered twice.

I do not know how to put the sword down. I use it every day, and I am good with it. But I have stopped believing it only cuts one way.

Every job a machine takes is a chair left empty somewhere else.

The sign said FULLY BOOKED. I hope that one of these Thursdays it is telling the truth.
