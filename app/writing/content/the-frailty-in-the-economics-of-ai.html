The neon sign beside the stage said FULLY BOOKED, and we were the only two people in the room.

I should explain that I plan a holiday the way other people do their taxes. For this one I had a document. It listed which exit to take out of which train station, and how much cash to change in Dhaka before the flight (never at the airport counter). The bars in it came off a list of the fifty best in Asia. So when we walked into one of the best rated places in Penang, on a Thursday evening in late September, an empty room was the one thing I had not planned for.

The chandelier was on. The bottles ran the whole length of one wall, lit red from behind. Somebody had folded napkins into the glasses on every table, and on the small stage at the far end there were music stands and a microphone set out for a band.

Every chair in the place was empty. We were the first customers they had seen all day.

It kept happening

At eight o'clock on a Friday we had the highest rated restaurant in Penang to ourselves. In Batu Ferringhi we were the only couple staying in the building, which is romantic for one evening and a little eerie by the second.

Kuala Lumpur had its quiet rooms too.

Up in the Cameron Highlands we stopped in the middle of the main road at dusk to take a photograph, and stood there a good while, and nothing came.

Even the tea had no queue, and this was at a tea plantation.

There is something unsettling about arriving, again and again, at a place that has plainly got itself ready for a crowd. The lights are on, the chairs are out, the To Let sign is up next door. You start to feel you have missed an announcement.

Who the chairs were for

The two of us are about as ordinary as tourists get. We are not rich. We travel on Bangladeshi passports, which meant a visa printed on a clean sheet of A4, and I had set us a careful budget for the week.

People like us are who those rooms were built for. Asia's whole tourism boom was a bet on a new middle class with a little money left over at the end of the year and a wish to see somewhere else. There were supposed to be more of us every year.

So I came home wondering where the rest of us were. I have an economics degree, and this is the sort of question it leaves you unable to drop.

Two things have happened this year to people who travel on what is left over.

The first is that getting anywhere became expensive. When the war between the United States and Iran began, jet fuel in Singapore more than doubled within a week, to a record of about $230 a barrel, and the airlines did what airlines do with a bill like that.

The second is quieter and I think it matters more. Technology companies cut more than 206,000 jobs this year while spending something like $700 billion on AI. One in every five job cuts announced in America named AI as the reason. Those are the announced ones.

I know where some of those jobs went

It would be more comfortable to write the rest of this about other people.

I run product operations for a startup, on my own. This past year, with an AI doing the typing, I have also been the data scientist, the engineer, the designer, the person who checks the engineer's work, and a few others I am too embarrassed to list. I wrote a whole article about it last week. One line of it has been bothering me ever since. I had written that a few years ago each of those jobs would have been a different person.

I meant it as a boast.

Our service team now handles 2.8 times the work it did in January, with the same nine or so people. Nobody lost a job over that. But nobody got one either, and when I try to picture the people who would have been hired, I find I can do it quite easily. They would be about thirty. They would have a salary, and two weeks of leave, and a document of their own with a list of bars in it. I am sitting in their chairs. All of them, at once.

I cannot claim those particular people would have turned up in Penang. What I can say is that every company is doing the same arithmetic I did, all in the same year, and somebody's missing hires would have.

For what it is worth, my laptop keeps a log of what the help costs me. Four days of it came to $114.37 at the published rates. I would hire it over me, too.

An old mistake

Economists have had a name for this for a long time. They call it the fallacy of composition, which is a stiff way of saying that something sensible for one of us can be ruinous when all of us do it at once. If I stand up at a football match I see better. If everyone stands, nobody does, and now we are all standing.

One company that replaces a role with software is being careful with its money. Every company doing it together is slowly getting rid of its own customers, because the wage it saved was going to be spent in somebody else's shop.

There is a story about the American union leader Walter Reuther being shown around a newly automated car plant in the 1950s. An executive, enjoying himself, asked how Reuther planned to collect union dues from the machines. Reuther asked him how he planned to sell them cars.

That exchange is seventy years old, and I think it is the whole of what I mean by frailty. We are getting very good at making things, and getting worse, at the same speed, at paying the people who were going to buy them.

Why Penang hears about it first

Nobody skips the rent to keep the holiday. A trip is paid for out of whatever is left, so it is the first thing a nervous household cancels, and the towns that live on trips find out before anyone else does.

In Malaysia tourism employs more than one working person in five.

On paper the visitors did come. International arrivals are up 2.4% this year. But roughly half of those are Singaporeans crossing a causeway, which is closer to a commute than a holiday, and the figure counts people through a gate. It cannot tell you who stayed the night.

In September, the month we were there, Malaysians flying around their own country were down 21.8% on the year before. That is the local middle class, the very people a small hotel in Batu Ferringhi depends on, deciding to stay at home.

And the hotels, which kept on building, filled 51.2% of their rooms in the first half of this year. In the same months of 2025 it was 54.1%.

What I expect, for whatever a forecast is worth

I work with forecasts for a living, so I know roughly how much weight to put on one. Here is mine anyway.

I do not think this arrives as a crash. A crash would at least get reported. I think it arrives as Thursdays. The long weekends will still sell out and somebody will photograph the crowds, while the ordinary weeks between them get a little thinner each year, until a bar that used to open six nights opens four.

I think most of the lost jobs will never look like losses. There will be no layoff to point at, only a vacancy that was never advertised, and it is very hard to be angry on behalf of a job nobody was ever offered. It has started already. Researchers at Stanford find employment in the work most exposed to AI running 19% below comparable jobs. I worked nine campus jobs while I was at university in Minnesota. I have been going through them in my head, trying to work out how many would still be given to a nineteen year old today, and I do not have an answer I trust.

And I think travel stays dear for a while. The United Nations' tourism agency began the year expecting growth of 3% to 4%. It now expects 1% to 2%.

None of this means the technology is a mistake. I use it every day, I am better at my work because of it, and I could not put it down now if I tried. But I used to think of what it saved me as free. I no longer do. Somebody is paying for it, a long way from my desk, and I happened to walk into their bar.

Every job a machine takes is a chair left empty somewhere else.

The sign said FULLY BOOKED. I would like to go back one Thursday and find it telling the truth.
