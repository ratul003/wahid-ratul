The neon sign beside the stage said FULLY BOOKED.

It was a Thursday evening in late September, in Penang. The bar was one of the best rated on the island. The chandelier was lit, the bottles ran the length of the wall, and napkins stood folded in glasses on the tables. On the little stage the music stands and a microphone were set out for a band. We were the first customers it had seen all day.

At 8PM on a Friday, in the highest rated restaurant in Penang, we were the only table. In Batu Ferringhi we were the only couple staying in the building. And everywhere we walked, in Kuala Lumpur too, there were To Let signs, hanging from what felt like every other building. Up in the Cameron Highlands it was quieter still.

I came home with a theory

It was a good one. I have an economics degree and it shows.

The war in Ukraine had dragged on for years. Then came the war between the United States and Iran, and fuel went through the roof. And on top of that, the AI boom: layoffs, hiring freezes, and a middle class, the people who take exactly this kind of trip, quietly cancelling it.

Wars, fuel, AI. Three causes, one empty bar. I was ready to write it.

Then I looked up the numbers, which is the half of my job I am supposed to do first.

Malaysia recorded 32.3 million international arrivals between January and September, up 2.4% on last year. Penang's airport carried 5.3% more international passengers in September, the month I was there, than a year earlier. The tourists I had declared missing were, on the official count, all present.

Parts of my theory survived. Jet fuel in Singapore did hit a record, about $230 a barrel, and Thailand's arrivals are down 4.6% this year. But crude oil never reached its 2008 peak, and I could find nothing connecting AI layoffs to anyone's holiday in Penang. Malaysia's visitors come mostly from Singapore, China and Indonesia. About half of them are Singaporeans crossing a causeway.

So what had I walked into?

Domestic air travel in Malaysia fell 21.8% in September, and Penang's hotels live on domestic guests. The island has gone from 14,000 registered hotel rooms in 2022 to 17,835 now. More beds, fewer of the people who usually fill them.

And the ones who did come all came at once.

Penang was not dead. Penang was full on the days I was not there.

The To Let signs I cannot explain, and I tried. Rents on heritage shophouses are reportedly up about 20% since 2024, so some of those signs may be landlords asking for more. I do not know.

A word from the mortality tables

Statistics has a name for the mistake I made, and for the one the tourism board is making in the other direction.

In 1979 three demographers, James Vaupel, Kenneth Manton and Eric Stallard, had a problem with death rates. Mortality at very old ages rose more slowly than anyone's biology said it should, as if people got tougher after 90.

They did not. The population was changing underneath the curve. Some people are more likely to die at every age, and they die first. What is left at 95 is a tougher crowd than the one that started, so the average flattens while every individual's risk keeps climbing.

They called that hidden, unequal risk frailty. The lesson has stayed in the field ever since: you cannot read an individual off a population curve.

Penang is a gentler cousin of that, I admit. Nobody was selected out of the data. Quiet Thursdays and packed long weekends were simply added together and divided. But the error is the same one. Plus 2.4% is real, and no bar owner on the island is living it.

I had gone looking for a shock that hit everyone. What I found was an average with a long tail, and I had been sitting in the tail with a drink.

Which row am I?

Then I did what I should have done before blaming AI for an empty bar. I checked what AI costs me.

My laptop keeps a log of every conversation I have with Claude Code. I added it up. Four days: about 181 million tokens read, a little under a million written. At list price, the pay-as-you-go rate, that is $114.37.

The seat I work from is a flat fee, and it does not move when I do. The most expensive seat on a Claude Team plan lists at about $125 a month. At the pace in that log, I would get through roughly $850 of list-price work in the same month.

In Penang I was the customer the average said was plentiful. At my desk I am the other kind of outlier, the Friday night that somebody else's quiet Tuesday pays for.

It turns out the whole industry is priced that way.

The average user

A flat AI subscription is a bet on the average subscriber. Most people ask a few questions a day, and their $20 or $200 covers it comfortably.

In June, SemiAnalysis bought every tier from OpenAI and Anthropic and ran them flat out on long coding tasks. The report is paywalled, so I am going on the coverage of it, which is consistent: a fully used $200 ChatGPT plan pulled about $14,000 a month of usage at list price, and the equivalent Claude plan about $8,000. On the top plans the margin is said to reach zero when a subscriber uses somewhere between 6% and 10% of what they are allowed.

List price is not what it costs a lab to serve anyone, me included. The real cost is lower, and nobody outside knows by how much. The shape survives the caveat. The light users carry the heavy ones.

The average chip

Depreciation is, I promise, the interesting part.

Every data centre has to assume how long a chip stays useful. Meta says five and a half years. Microsoft and Alphabet say six. Michael Burry says the economic life is closer to two or three, and that the gap understates the industry's depreciation by about $176 billion between 2026 and 2028. He has not published his working, and rental prices for older chips, reported this month, cut against him.

Both sides are arguing over one number, and here the word fits exactly: a chip's useful life is a survival curve. Some will earn rent for seven years on dull workloads. Some will be stranded in three. Six years is an average over a fleet.

The average quarter

The cloud giants get quoted as one balance sheet. Last quarter Alphabet ran free cash flow of minus $9.1 billion. Microsoft, in the same race, was plus $18.6 billion.

And they hold each other up. Nvidia put $30 billion into OpenAI and guaranteed up to $105 billion for one of its data centres. OpenAI buys compute from Oracle. Oracle buys chips from Nvidia.

Survival analysis has a name for that as well: shared frailty. It is the term you add when the individuals in a study are not independent, when one family's bad year is every member's bad year. In a market it means the risks you were averaging over do not average out.

The principle

Every price built on an average is a loan from the light users to the heavy ones. Loans get called.

I do not think that makes AI a bubble, any more than one empty bar made Penang dead. A bubble is a claim about everyone at once. Frailty is a claim about who goes first: the heaviest users on the flattest plans, the chips with the shortest real lives, the balance sheet that needed every quarter to be an average one. It has started. In October the same firm found that OpenAI had roughly halved what its $200 plan delivers. Nobody announced the end of a subsidy. The allowance simply moved.

So find out which side of the average you are on, before the people who set the price do.

The sign said FULLY BOOKED. On average, it probably is.
