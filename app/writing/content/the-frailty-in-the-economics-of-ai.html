The neon sign beside the stage said FULLY BOOKED.

It was lying.

It was a Thursday evening in late September, in Penang. The bar was one of the best rated on the island. The chandelier was lit, the bottles ran the length of the wall, and napkins stood folded in glasses on the tables. On the little stage the music stands and a microphone were set out for a band that, like everyone else, had not arrived. We were the first customers it had seen all day. I got the impression we might also be the last.

At 8PM on a Friday, in the highest rated restaurant in Penang, we were the only table. The service was extraordinary. It would be, with a ratio like that.

In Batu Ferringhi we were the only couple staying in the building, which is romantic for exactly one evening and then starts to feel like the opening of a film that does not end well.

Everywhere we walked there were To Let signs, hanging from what felt like every other building. Kuala Lumpur had its quiet rooms too.

Up in the Cameron Highlands it was quieter still.

Even the tea had no queue.

Two blades

I have an economics degree, so I did what we do. I drew a line from an empty bar to the global economy before the ice had melted.

I still think the line is right. It has two blades.

The first is fuel. When the war between the United States and Iran began, jet fuel in Singapore rose 140% in a week and touched about $230 a barrel, a record. Airlines doubled their fuel surcharges. UN Tourism, which had expected international travel to grow 3% to 4% this year, cut that to 1% to 2%. In the first half of 2026 the whole world managed 0.4%, and the second quarter went backwards. Thailand's arrivals are down 4.6% this year. Asia is still 11% short of where it was in 2019.

A holiday that costs more gets taken by fewer people. That part is not complicated.

The second blade is the one I work with every day.

The blade I am holding

This year technology companies cut more than 206,000 jobs while putting an estimated $700 billion into AI infrastructure. Of all the job cuts announced in the United States through October, 121,401 named AI as the reason, about one in five. Stanford researchers, as reported, find employment in the occupations most exposed to AI running 19% below comparable fields.

I am not reading about this from a safe distance. I am one of the reasons.

I run product operations for a startup, alone, with no dedicated engineer. Last week I published an article about it. This year, with AI doing the typing, I have been the product manager, the data scientist, the data engineer, the designer, the quality analyst and three other people. I wrote, with some pride, that a few years ago each of those was a different person. Our service team now handles 2.8 times the customer moves it handled in January, with the same nine or so agents.

Nobody was laid off. Nobody was hired either.

My laptop tells me what the help costs. Four days of it, about 181 million tokens of reading and writing, comes to $114.37 at list price. There is no salary on earth that competes with that, and I say so as someone who would quite like to keep his.

So count the people who did not get those jobs. They are not on a flight to Penang. They are not the second table at the highest rated restaurant on the island.

The fallacy

Economics has a name for this, and it is older than any of the technology. It is the fallacy of composition: what is sensible for one is ruinous when everyone does it.

One company replacing a role with software is being prudent. Every company doing it at once is quietly deleting its own customers, because every wage it saves was somebody else's revenue.

The union leader Walter Reuther used to tell a story about touring a newly automated car plant in the 1950s. An executive pointed at the machines and asked how he planned to collect union dues from them. Reuther asked how the company planned to get them to buy cars. (The version where the executive is Henry Ford II is better known and almost certainly embroidered. The question survives either way.)

That is the frailty in the economics of AI. We are getting better at making things at exactly the rate we are getting worse at paying the people who buy them. It is progress, and it is also the other thing. A sword with two edges, and we are holding it by the blade.

Why the bar goes first

Tourism is what people buy with what is left over. Nobody cancels the rent to keep the holiday. So when incomes thin and fares rise, the trip is the first thing to go, and a place that lives on trips is the first to feel it.

Malaysia is that kind of place. Tourism industries are 15.9% of its economy and employ 3.7 million people, 22.1% of everyone with a job. A bar in George Town is not a side effect of the economy. It is the economy.

Statisticians, my other tribe, use the word frailty for a hidden weakness that decides who goes first when a population is put under strain. The average keeps looking healthy for a surprisingly long time, because the frail drop out quietly and the survivors are the ones still being counted.

What the numbers will and will not let me say

I would be a poor analyst if I stopped there, so here is the part that argues with me.

Malaysia recorded 32.3 million international arrivals from January to September, up 2.4% on last year. Penang's airport carried 5.3% more international passengers in September, the month I was there. On the headline, tourism is not dying. It is fine.

Then look at who is being counted. About half of those arrivals are Singaporeans crossing a causeway, which is less a holiday than a commute with better food. And domestic air travel, Malaysians flying around their own country, fell 21.8% in September. The local middle class is the customer Penang's hotels depend on, and it stayed home.

The hotels show it, once you compare like with like.

Occupancy rose from 54.9% in 2024 to 56.8% in 2025. Then the first half of 2026 came in at 51.2%, against 54.1% for the same months a year earlier. The number of rooms never fell. It went up every year, from 343,866 to 350,069. In Penang the hotel association's registered rooms have grown from 14,000 in 2022 to 17,835, and its chairman put occupancy at about 60% in August.

More rooms, fewer of them filled, in the year the fuel went up and the hiring slowed.

Now the honest limits. I cannot show you a line from a redundancy in San Francisco to an empty table in Penang. Nobody has that data. Some of what I saw is Penang building hotels faster than it can fill them. The Kuala Lumpur lane below was busy, and I took the photo myself. And the economists who went looking for a spike in graduate unemployment this summer did not find one.

So I am not claiming a measurement. I am claiming a direction, with a mechanism behind it and early numbers that lean the same way. That is weaker than I would like and stronger than a hunch.

The principle

Every job a machine takes is also a customer some other business loses.

We keep the two ledgers in different buildings. One has the productivity gain, the 2.8 times, the $114 that did four days of work. The other has an empty bar with a band's microphone set out for nobody. They are the same transaction, entered twice.

I do not know how to put the sword down. I use it every day and I am good with it. But I have stopped believing it only cuts one way.

The sign said FULLY BOOKED. The spreadsheet that paid for the sign probably agreed.
